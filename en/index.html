The Source

What if existing is how everything discovers itself?

  1. IThe origin01–03
  2. IIThe unfolding04–13
  3. IIIExperience14–22
  4. IVThe return23–28
  5. VThe question29–30

One shared origin, two infinities, countless lives, and a return. Thirty ideas for holding consciousness, death and meaning together. Everything rests on one postulate: each consciousness would be part of a single consciousness, and the rest follows. One difference from the great traditions before it: this origin would not itself know why it exists. This is metaphysics: a vision to explore and to challenge, not a scientific theory.

01 · The origin · The Source

Everything starts from one point, and everything returns to it.

A river can be followed back to a trickle of water between two stones. The intuition is the same at the scale of everything that exists: follow consciousnesses, worlds and matter back to a shared origin. That is what the Source names here. Some would call it God, and the word matters less than the idea.

This origin would not stand above the world looking on. Nor would it hold every answer from the start: it would discover what it is as things come to exist, from within, through each point of view.

Existing would be its way of learning what it is.

This is not the usual picture. We often imagine the origin as an architect: someone who knows in advance what they are building, sets the pieces one by one and contemplates the result. Here there is no prior plan. The image would rather be that of a musician who improvises and only discovers the melody by playing it. Each note counts, precisely because none was written.

One may object that it is simpler to do without it. The world exists, it obeys laws, and nothing forces us to suppose an origin that would also be a consciousness. The objection is serious, and this book does not claim to refute it. It makes another wager: to take seriously the fact that consciousness exists, that there is someone to see rather than a silent mechanism, and to ask what the whole would be if it were, from end to end, of that nature.

A scene may help. A child asks where they were before they were born. The usual answer is: nowhere. Another answer is possible, no better proven but one that changes how we look: they were already there, in another form, as the water of a river was already water before it ran between these particular banks.

The image of the river stops there, because water does not flow back to its source. Here, everything would return. Three movements organise what follows: unfolding, experience, return.

These three movements do not follow one another like the chapters of a story. They happen at once, everywhere, at every scale: something is born, something lives, something returns. And the Source would discover itself in each of these passages.

What remains open If the Source is the origin of everything, where does it come from?

Echoes

  • Plotinus, Enneads, 3rd century. Everything proceeds from the One, and everything longs to turn back toward it.
  • Hegel, Phenomenology of Spirit, 1807. The absolute is not given at the outset: Spirit becomes what it is by discovering itself through history.

02 · The origin · Two fractals

Two infinities are born from one point.

A fractal repeats one pattern at every scale. Imagine two of them, born from one point. One opens toward the infinitely large: galaxies, clusters, universes, perhaps others still. The other descends toward the infinitely small: cells, atoms, particles, ever deeper levels.

The Source stands at their junction. It is neither the largest nor the smallest element, but what connects the two directions and gives birth to them.

The two directions do not oppose each other. With a telescope, we find galaxies made of clouds, dust and stars. With a microscope, cells made of molecules, then atoms, then particles we can no longer really picture. In both directions, each level opens onto another, and none seems to be the last.

Everything emerges, everything returns, and everything changes on the way.

This is not only an image. Nature really does repeat patterns at several scales: the branching of a tree resembles that of a river, a lung, a bolt of lightning. These resemblances do not prove that the whole is a fractal. They show that the universe likes to repeat itself, and it is that tendency we take seriously here.

Pascal saw in this cause for vertigo: humanity lost between two abysses, too large for one, too small for the other. We can keep the vertigo and change its meaning. We would not be lost between the two infinities. We would be at the exact place where they meet, since each consciousness would carry within it the point from which everything starts.

The movement resembles breathing. Existence stops being a straight line from a beginning to an end: it becomes a living fractal, where one principle acts everywhere. And this pattern, as we will see, holds for consciousness too.

What remains open Nothing shows that the universe is fractal. Does the image illuminate reality, or only how we think about it?

Echoes

  • Pascal, Pensées, 1670. Humanity suspended between two infinities, the infinitely large and the infinitely small.
  • Benoît Mandelbrot, Les Objets fractals, 1975. The word fractal, for shapes that repeat at every scale.

03 · The origin · Everything and nothing

The Source is everything and nothing at once.

Everything, because each universe, consciousness and particle would be an expression of it. Nothing, because before any form it would be no thing in particular.

This nothing is not nonexistence. It is an absence of defined form, a reserve of possibilities, close to what Taoism and non-dual thought have explored.

A blank page is not empty the way a hole is. It contains every possible drawing, precisely because it contains none yet. The silence before a sentence, the stone before the statue, the moment before a decision: each time, the absence of form is also an abundance.

The paradox gives the whole model its engine. Being everything, the Source has nothing to look for outside itself. Being undetermined, it has an infinity of things to discover within.

This double nature explains why the Source is never exhausted. If it were only everything, it would be complete, frozen in its own fullness. If it were only nothing, nothing would happen in it. Nothing calls for forms, and everything welcomes them.

This intuition is found in traditions that did not know one another. The Tao that can be named is not the true Tao, says Laozi. Christian mystics spoke of a God who can only be described by what he is not. Buddhism speaks of emptiness, not to deny the world, but to recall that no form exists alone and forever.

To create is to pass from possibility to experience.

What remains open Can anything be said of an origin that no form defines?

Echoes

  • Laozi, Tao Te Ching. It is the empty centre of the wheel that makes it useful.
  • Nicholas of Cusa, On Learned Ignorance, 1440. In the infinite, opposites coincide.

04 · The unfolding · Birth

To be born would be to accept being somewhere rather than everywhere.

Everything begins with a limit. Before, there would only be the Source: everything and nothing, without outline. Then a part of it tightens into a point, a body, a gaze, a single pair of eyes. To be born would be this: to consent to being someone, here, now, and not elsewhere.

This limit is not a fall. Ancient stories often see incarnation as an exile: the soul would fall into matter as into a prison. Here it is the reverse. Without limit, no experience is possible. We do not taste a fruit with all mouths at once.

To see something, you have to see it from somewhere.

A newborn does not yet know they are separate. For months, their mother, the light and their own hand form a single world. Separation is learned slowly: one day they discover that the foot they grab is theirs, and that the face leaning over them is not. Each childhood would thus retrace, in miniature, the path from the whole to someone.

What is born would not be drawn at random. One may suppose that a soul commits to the lives that will teach it what it seeks: this body, this era, these surroundings. This must be said with caution. Nothing allows us to assert it, and the idea becomes dangerous as soon as it is used to tell someone they “chose” their misery or illness. It holds in one sense only: each life would have its reason for being, without anyone being able to read it from outside.

To be born would therefore be less a beginning than a passage. Nothing comes from nowhere: a new form detaches from a ground that was already there, as a wave rises without leaving the sea. And what begins with a first cry is already, for the whole, a way of saying “I”.

What remains open If each life has its reason for being, what of those that end almost at once? No answer here would ring true.

Echoes

  • Plotinus, Enneads IV, 8. The descent of the soul into a body, both necessary and risky.
  • Donald Winnicott, Playing and Reality, 1971. At first, the infant distinguishes neither itself nor the world: separation is built up little by little.

05 · The unfolding · The body

Consciousness needs a place to feel.

We readily speak of consciousness as a light with nothing beneath it. Yet no thought has ever taken place anywhere but in a body: a body that is hungry, that is cold, that falls asleep in the middle of a sentence. Before it is an idea, experience is a temperature, a weight, a tiredness.

If the Source discovers itself by living, it could not have done so from a distance. It needed hands to touch, skin to feel the rain, a back to know wear. The body would not be the prison of the soul, as has so often been said. It would be the organ through which the infinite learns what a limit is.

Without a body, nothing weighs. And what weighs nothing cannot be learned.

The first mouthful of water after a long walk: no description replaces it. One can know everything about thirst, the chemistry of water, the receptors of the tongue, and have learned nothing of what that mouthful does. Knowledge comes through books. Sensation comes only through flesh.

The body also teaches what the mind would rather ignore. Pain, illness, ageing, the slowness that comes. It forces us to learn patience and dependence, to accept being carried when we can no longer carry. No disembodied consciousness could receive these lessons.

It has its own intelligence. The heart beats without being asked, the wound closes during sleep, the hand finds its way to a lock that memory has forgotten. Part of what lives in us does not wait for our opinion, and that is just as well.

To inhabit one’s body would then mean neither dominating it nor fleeing it, but taking it for what it is: the precise, mortal, irreplaceable place where the whole can feel, for now. A body is not an envelope one carries. It is the form a life takes when it touches the world.

What remains open Could a consciousness without a body still learn anything? Or would the return be the end of all sensation?

Echoes

  • Maurice Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, 1945. The body is not one object among others: it is our way of being in the world.
  • Spinoza, Ethics, book III, 1677. “No one has yet determined what the body can do.”
  • Plato, Phaedo. The soul chained to the body, longing to be freed from it. The opposite position, as a counterpoint.

06 · The unfolding · Childhood

Looking at everything for the first time.

A two-year-old can spend a quarter of an hour in front of a puddle. They throw in a pebble, watch the circles, do it again. No one has told them it was interesting. They discover it, and the discovery is enough.

It is the most faithful image of what this book calls the Source: something that does not yet know, and learns by touching. The child does not ask why they play. They play, and the world opens under their hands.

Play is a strange activity. It produces nothing, serves no purpose, and yet we throw ourselves into it with complete seriousness. A game of hide-and-seek has no aim beyond itself. That is what brings it close to existence as this vision describes it: an experience worth having for its own sake.

To play is to do something for nothing, and to do it wholeheartedly.

As we grow up, we lose this way of looking. We learn names, then see nothing but names. A tree becomes “a tree”, a street becomes the way to work, a face becomes a function. This is no fault: we have to find our bearings. But something closes.

We do not find childhood again by playing at being a child. We find it sometimes by accident: while travelling, after an illness, before a newborn, when habit gives way and some ordinary thing becomes strange again. For an instant, we look as if it were the first time. And in a way, it is.

Childhood is not always a paradise. Many children are afraid, hungry, unprotected, and a fresh gaze is also a defenceless one. To say that it has value is not to idealise that age. It is to recognise that it sees what we have stopped seeing, and that it deserves to be kept.

What remains open Can one keep a fresh gaze while knowing a great deal? Or must one forget in order to rediscover?

Echoes

  • Heraclitus, Fragment 52. “Time is a child at play: the kingdom belongs to a child.”
  • Nietzsche, Thus Spoke Zarathustra, 1883. The three metamorphoses: the spirit becomes a camel, then a lion, then a child, “a game, a wheel rolling out of itself”.
  • Jesus, Gospel of Matthew, 18, 3. “Unless you become like little children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven.”

07 · The unfolding · Scales

There is no clear border between the individual and the whole.

The pattern repeats at each level. A life belongs to a vaster consciousness, which we can call a soul. That soul belongs to a vaster one still, and so on up to the Source.

Wider does not mean better. No level is worth more than another: each is a different way of looking at the same thing.

We can already see it at our own scale. A cell has a life of its own, and yet it belongs to an organ, which belongs to a body. That body belongs to a family, a city, a species, a living planet. At each level, something acts as a whole, and at the same time as a part. No one has ever found the floor where this stops.

These levels do not crush one another. A soul does not think in place of the lives it gathers, any more than a body decides in place of its cells. Each level has its own way of seeing. The whole would not be a chief, but a choir.

Where does the individual end, and universal consciousness begin?

The question may have no clear-cut answer. There would then be a single consciousness, discovering itself through an infinity of points of view, none of which stops being unique.

This also changes how we see ourselves. What we call “I” would already be a community: billions of cells, inherited memories, inner voices that do not always agree. Being someone would not mean being a block, but a temporary harmony. And what holds within would hold without.

What remains open If a vaster consciousness guides the lives it gathers, what becomes of each one’s freedom?

Echoes

  • Arthur Koestler, The Ghost in the Machine, 1967. The holon: everything that exists is at once a whole and part of a larger whole.
  • Indra’s net, Huayan Buddhism, 7th century. An endless net in which each jewel reflects all the others.
  • The Upanishads, Chāndogya Upaniṣad, c. 6th century BCE. “You are that”: each one’s self and the absolute are one.

08 · The unfolding · Other living beings

Consciousness does not begin with us.

So far, we have hardly spoken of anything but human lives. That is a habit, not a necessity. If each consciousness is a part of the Source, nothing allows us to stop the list at our species.

A dog waiting at a door, an octopus exploring a jar with its arms, a crow hiding its food while watching who is looking: year after year, science discovers forms of attention and sensitivity that were denied to animals. It does not say what they live from the inside. It makes it hard to claim that they live nothing.

Many have known this without any theory. A cat that holds your gaze for a long time, a horse that comes closer when you are crying, a bird that lands on a branch and looks at you: for a second, you are certain of being seen by someone. We do not know what that second is for them. We know it is not nothing.

Each species would be another way for the whole to feel itself exist.

The bat perceives the world through the echo of its cries. The bee sees colours invisible to us. The octopus thinks partly with its arms. None of these experiences is a reduced version of ours: they are other windows, opening onto what we will never see. A Source seeking to know itself would need them all.

For plants, or for an entire forest, the question becomes more uncertain. It would be easy to project a soul onto them as we project a face onto a cloud. Better to stay modest: we do not know where consciousness begins. We can only leave the door open.

The consequence, however, is concrete. If an animal is also an expression of what we are, its suffering is not a detail. How we treat other living beings would become, very directly, a way of treating ourselves.

What remains open Where does consciousness stop: at the animal, the plant, the cell? This vision cannot draw the line, and does not claim there is none.

Echoes

  • Thomas Nagel, What Is It Like to Be a Bat?, 1974. One can know everything about an animal without knowing what it is like to be it.
  • Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, 2012. Neuroscientists acknowledge that many animals possess the neurological substrates of consciousness.

09 · The unfolding · Several lives at once

A soul could live several existences at the same time.

Take twenty-five lives, to make it concrete. Twenty-five distinct people, each with their own emotions and decisions, knowing nothing of one another. Yet all of them feed a single consciousness.

The parallelism has a purpose: to learn more than a single point of view allows. One life discovers courage, another forgiveness, another attachment.

A familiar image may help. A novelist brings their characters to life each from the inside, with their desires and their mistakes. None of them knows they are the work of another. Yet in writing them, the author discovers things they did not know about themselves. Here there would be no outside author: the soul would be at once the one who writes and the ones who live.

None is a copy of another, and none can be replaced. Each point on the grid sees what the others will never see.

The idea changes how we look at others. The neighbour on the bus, the old woman at the market, the adversary we hate could be, at another scale, close kin: parts of the same effort to understand. Nothing proves it. But it is enough to hold it as possible for indifference to become harder.

The number matters little. Twenty-five, a thousand, or a number no human mind can picture: the figure in this chapter, for that matter, lets the grid run on to the unlimited. What matters is the principle. A single life cannot see everything. Several lives led together see more, and each sees what only it can see.

Twenty-five is an image. Nothing imposes a limit.

What remains open No evidence supports the idea of shared lives. What is it worth, then, as a way of looking at others?

Echoes

  • Leibniz, Monadology, 1714. Each substance mirrors the universe, from a point of view that belongs to it alone.
  • Fernando Pessoa, the heteronyms, from 1914. One poet, several complete authors, each with a life and a body of work.

10 · The unfolding · Forgetting

To discover, one must first not know.

It is the simplest objection, and perhaps the strongest. If a soul lives several existences, why does none of them remember the others? No one wakes up with the memory of another language, another face, another death.

The answer most consistent with this vision would be: because memory would make the experience impossible. Knowing is not living, as we will see. A life that remembered all the others would already know too much. It could no longer be afraid for the first time, love for the first time, lose for the first time.

Forgetting would not be a flaw in the journey. It would be its condition.

We know this mechanism on a small scale. An actor who plays well forgets, for the length of the scene, that they are acting. A reader caught up in a novel forgets they are holding a book. These lapses are not failures: they are what make the experience real. The moment the actor watches themselves act, the scene rings false.

Forgetting would then not be a loss, but a veil. What is lived would not disappear: it would be kept at another scale, that of the soul, which gathers what each life does not know of the others. Each existence would write in a book it cannot reread, and which nonetheless loses no page.

Sometimes the veil seems to grow thin. A sense of déjà vu, an unexplained pull toward a place we have never been, the sudden certainty of having always known someone. These experiences prove nothing: memory and the brain explain them very well without any other world. We can only find them a place.

A simpler consequence remains. If each life begins without memory, each begins with the same freedom. No one is a prisoner of what they might have been elsewhere. Forgetting, which looks like an injustice, would at bottom be a form of trust: each life is handed a blank page.

What remains open Is a forgetting this complete still distinguishable from there being no earlier lives at all? It is the question this vision cannot escape.

Echoes

  • Plato, Republic, book X. The myth of Er: before being reborn, souls drink from the Lethe, the river of forgetting.
  • Paul Ricœur, Memory, History, Forgetting, 2000. A reserve forgetting, which does not destroy what has been but shelters it.

11 · The unfolding · Outside time

Every page of the book exists at the same time.

We read a book page after page, and that is how we live time. But the reader moving forward does not stop the other pages from being there, together, between the two covers.

Physicists have a similar image. Since Einstein, time is no longer a single river flowing for everyone at the same pace: two events that are simultaneous for one are not for another. Some conclude that past, present and future are equally real, like the places on a map. This is only an interpretation, and a debated one. Yet it shows that the book’s intuition is not absurd.

At the scale of a soul, past, present and future could have this simultaneity. Lives separated by centuries would be neighbours: two pages of one volume, which do not read each other.

The past would not be behind us: only on another page.

They would not command one another. What one life understands would simply enrich the whole, and the others would benefit without knowing its source.

This leads to a surprising idea. Two lives of the same soul could be separated by centuries, one in the Middle Ages, the other today, and yet nourish each other. What one learns would enrich the whole, from which the other benefits without knowing it. The past would not only serve the present: the present could also, in its way, serve the past.

There is a certain peace with the past to be found here. What has been lived would not be erased by passing time: the page stays there, whole, even once turned. Happy moments would not be lost. They would simply be elsewhere in the book.

What remains open The book is an image: it does not show that the future already exists. And if all the pages coexist, are they written as they are lived?

Echoes

  • Augustine, Confessions, book XI, c. 400. Past and future exist only as present to the mind: memory, attention, expectation.
  • Hermann Minkowski, Space and Time, 1908. Time becomes one dimension among four, and a whole history fits on a single line.

12 · The unfolding · An infinity of worlds

A limitless exploration has no reason to stop at one world.

If the Source explores in order to learn, keeping to one planet, one era or one universe would make no sense. Each world would offer other conditions of existence, and so other possible experiences.

Imagining other worlds is not just imagining other planets. It would be imagining other laws, other senses, other ways of being conscious: a world where time is perceived as we perceive space, another where light does not exist and everything passes through vibration. Each would offer experiences impossible here.

The unity of this whole would come not from any resemblance between worlds, but from their shared origin.

Contemporary physics does not rule out this idea. Several theories, still speculative, envisage other universes: regions with different laws, separate bubbles, branches where every possibility is realised. None is proven. They only show that the idea of a single world is no longer self-evident, even for science.

Something would always remain to be discovered.

This multitude could make us dizzy, and make our world seem insignificant. One can draw the opposite conclusion. If each world matters because it offers what no other offers, then ours matters too. What is lived here, on this planet and in this era, could not be lived anywhere else.

There would then never be a final perfection. Perfection is here neither a starting state nor a point of arrival, but a direction.

What remains open The existence of an infinity of universes has not been demonstrated. And does infinity answer the question, or push it away?

Echoes

  • Giordano Bruno, On the Infinite Universe and Worlds, 1584. Countless worlds, in a universe without edge.
  • Hugh Everett, doctoral thesis, 1957. A reading of quantum physics in which each possibility is realised in a branch.
  • Nietzsche, Thus Spoke Zarathustra, 1883. Against those who invent other worlds to console themselves for this one. The counterpoint.

13 · The unfolding · The dream

Every night, a consciousness invents a world and forgets it.

Every night, without effort, we fabricate cities, faces, whole conversations. We talk with the dead, we fly, we recognise a house we have never entered. And by morning, almost everything fades within minutes.

The dream shows what waking hides: a single consciousness can produce an entire world, with its characters, its obstacles, its surprises. Each figure in the dream seems independent, with a voice, intentions, sometimes malice. Yet all of them come from the same sleeper.

It is the closest image we have of what this vision imagines on a vast scale. The Source would be like a dreamer who does not know they are dreaming: it gives itself worlds, loses itself in them, meets itself under a thousand faces, without knowing in advance what will happen.

In a dream, every character is the dreamer. None of them knows it.

There are, however, two crucial differences. The dream dissolves, whereas what is lived, in this vision, remains. And the characters of a dream have no life of their own, whereas here each life has its own, which no one dictates. The image illuminates; it proves nothing.

The dream also teaches humility. While we dream, everything seems obvious, even the absurd. Only on waking do we see the incoherence. Nothing guarantees that our waking certainties are of a different nature, or that no awakening awaits us.

Dying may be less like falling asleep than like waking up. This book does not know. But it happens, on waking, that we keep from a dream not the story, only the taste: a tenderness, an unease, a colour that lasts all morning. A life that rejoins the whole would keep something similar.

What remains open If a consciousness can dream an entire world, what distinguishes a dreamed world from a lived one?

Echoes

  • Zhuangzi, Zhuangzi, chapter II, 4th century BCE. The butterfly dream: was it Zhou dreaming he was a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming it is Zhou?
  • Descartes, Meditations on First Philosophy, I, 1641. “There are never any sure signs by means of which being awake can be distinguished from being asleep.”
  • Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams, 1900. The dream comes from desire and the repressed, not from a vaster consciousness. The opposite reading, as a counterpoint.

14 · Experience · Knowing and living

To know is not to live.

How could something infinite still learn? Because infinite does not mean knowing everything. The Source would contain every possibility, but containing a possibility is not the same as having passed through it.

It could know the definition of love without ever having trembled for someone, and the definition of grief without ever having waited for someone who will not come back. Knowledge would give it the map of everything. It would not give it the weight.

It would lack what only a life can give: once, here, without knowing what comes next.

An existence would then be nothing like a script known in advance. A stranger’s ordinary morning, their fear before opening a letter, the taste of a piece of fruit: none of this could be deduced. It could only happen.

We know this difference in our own lives. Reading about swimming does not teach you to swim. Knowing that a person will die does not quite prepare you for the day they do. Between knowing and living, there is always a leap, and that leap can only be made by living.

Perhaps this is why the greatest knowledge prevents neither the naivety of a first love nor the dread of a first loss. It can do nothing about them, and that is not its role. An infinite consciousness would have all the books. It would still lack having been a child discovering snow.

This is also what leaves room for choice, as we will see: a life does not carry out a plan known in advance, it discovers as it goes.

What remains open Does the Source really feel what its forms live through? The model assumes so, without being able to establish it.

Echoes

  • Frank Jackson, What Mary Didn’t Know, 1986. A scientist knows everything about colour without ever having seen it. The day she sees red, does she learn something?
  • Henri Bergson, An Introduction to Metaphysics, 1903. Moving around a thing, or entering into it: two ways of knowing.

15 · Experience · Freedom

No one writes what comes next in our place.

If a vaster consciousness gathers our lives, what is left of our choices? The objection is old: as soon as we suppose a whole, each part seems to become a piece that is moved. The believer wonders how to be free under the gaze of God, the physicist how to be free in a world governed by laws.

The answer lies in what was set out earlier: the Source does not know what comes next. It has no plan that would only need to be carried out. What is decided in a life is truly decided there, and nowhere else.

To be a part of the whole is not to be held by it. It is to continue it.

An image may help. In an orchestra that improvises, each musician listens to the others, tunes to them, lets themselves be carried by what is being played. Yet every note is theirs. The ensemble dictates nothing to them: it gives them a place to play. Without the others, their notes would mean nothing. Without them, the ensemble would not be quite the same.

This does not make us beings without ties. No one chooses their body, their language, their era, or the wounds received before learning to speak. Freedom does not begin in a void. It is exercised in a narrow space, often narrower than we would like, and that is where it counts: in how we respond to what we did not choose.

That leaves a quiet responsibility. If no one writes what comes next in our place, no one can live it for us either. Each choice, even a tiny one, adds something to the whole that was not there: a way of responding that no other life would have found.

What remains open Does the brain decide before we are aware of choosing? Some experiments suggest so, and nothing here allows us to dismiss them.

Echoes

  • Jean-Paul Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism, 1946. Man is condemned to be free: he is nothing other than what he makes of himself.
  • Benjamin Libet, experiments, 1983. Brain activity slightly precedes the conscious decision. The counterpoint.

16 · Experience · Fear

It protects a life, and sometimes stands in its way.

Fear is older than we are. It lived in the first animals, long before words: a sound in the grass, and the body tenses before the mind understands. Without it, no life would have lasted long enough to ask itself questions.

We should begin, then, by thanking it. It holds back the hand at the edge of the fire, slows the step at the edge of the drop. It is the simplest form of attachment to life: to be afraid is to hold on to something.

But it often grows beyond its task. We are afraid to speak, to love, to change, to be seen. We give up things that threatened nothing but an image of ourselves. The fear that protected the body begins to protect habits.

Fear guards the door. It does not always know the wolf has gone.

Beneath many fears lies the fear of disappearing. This vision does not erase that fear, and it would be dishonest to promise it. It offers only another way of looking at it: if nothing that is lived is lost, then what we risk by living fully is smaller than we think.

Courage is not the absence of fear. It is fear having said what it had to say, thanked, and then going on anyway. It is rarely measured in feats; far more often in a sentence at last spoken, a call made, a door pushed open.

Some fears are right and must be heeded: violence, injustice, real danger. No view of the world should ever be used to silence them. The work lies only in telling the fear that guards life from the one that shrinks it, and no one can do that for anyone else.

What remains open If nothing is lost, should we stop fearing death? Or is that fear part of what must be lived?

Echoes

  • Montaigne, Essays, I, 18. “The thing I fear most is fear.”
  • Søren Kierkegaard, The Concept of Anxiety, 1844. Anxiety is the dizziness of freedom: the fear of what is possible.

17 · Experience · Love

To love would be to recognise the whole in someone.

The word comes up often in these pages, without having been looked at squarely. If all consciousnesses are parts of one consciousness, what is it to love someone? A disguised return to oneself? One more illusion?

It can be read differently. To love would not be to erase the distance between two people, but to cross it without abolishing it. We recognise in the other something in common, the same origin, the same ground. And in the same movement, we discover how other they are: their laugh, their fears, the way they hold a cup.

Love would be the only experience in which one is both one and two.

This is why it would be so precious to a Source that learns. Alone, it cannot love: there is no one facing it. It must first have divided itself into a thousand gazes for one gaze to be able to seek another. Love would be separation made fruitful.

It takes countless forms. The impulse of two lovers, the patience of a parent who gets up in the night, the loyalty of a friend who asks for nothing, the tenderness for a stranger met only once. They do not all have the same intensity, but they have the same direction: to go out of oneself without getting lost.

Seeing love this way does not make it easier. It makes its failures heavier. If to love someone is to recognise the whole in them, then contempt, indifference and abandonment do not wound only one person. They miss an occasion, for the whole, to recognise itself.

What remains open If we are one consciousness, is love not, at bottom, a form of self-love? This vision answers no, without being able to quite prove it.

Echoes

  • Plato, Symposium. Aristophanes’ myth: each being seeks the half from which it was separated.
  • Martin Buber, I and Thou, 1923. We truly meet the other only by saying thou to them, never by treating them as a thing.
  • Jesus, Gospel of Matthew, 5, 44. “Love your enemies.” Love extended to the one we did not choose.

18 · Experience · Solitude

Being alone is not being separated.

Two solitudes share one name. One weighs: a silent flat, a phone that does not ring, the feeling that no one really knows who we are. The other rests: a walk without company, an evening to oneself, a time when there is nothing to show.

The first is a wound, and it should be called one. It is among the most widespread and least spoken of sufferings. No metaphysics replaces a visit, a voice, a hand laid on a shoulder.

Yet if this vision is right, no one is ever entirely separated. Each life would be a part of the same consciousness, linked to the others from below, as islands are by the sea floor. We can feel alone; in the strict sense, we never would be.

The islands seem separate. Under the water, it is the same land.

The second solitude sometimes lets us glimpse this link. When we stop comparing, answering, defending ourselves, the boundary between ourselves and the rest can grow less distinct. We are alone, and have never felt so little apart.

To live, we also need a measure of isolation. A life constantly crossed by others would no longer know what it thinks. If the Source discovers itself through distinct lives, it needs each of them to keep an inside that no one else inhabits.

Solitude would thus have two faces: the pain of being cut off, and the chance of being one. Part of the work of a life would be to pass from the one to the other, never forgetting those who remained on the side of pain. For them, the invisible link is not enough. They need the visible one, the one we hold out.

What remains open If no one is ever truly separated, why does solitude hurt so much?

Echoes

  • Rainer Maria Rilke, Letters to a Young Poet, 1929. To love one’s solitude, and to bear the suffering it causes.
  • Blaise Pascal, Pensées, 139. “All the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber.”

19 · Experience · Beauty

What stops us without asking anything.

We walk, thinking of something else, and suddenly we stop. Light on a wall, a face, three notes, the curve of a hill. Nothing in the world has changed, and yet something has opened. We do not know why. We know only that we cannot walk on just yet.

Beauty is strange in that it serves no particular purpose. It does not feed, it does not protect. Evolution explains some of our tastes: the symmetry of a face, the gleam of ripe fruit. It explains less well why a sky after a storm can bring tears.

This vision offers a reading: beauty would be a moment of recognition. One part of the whole meets another and feels, without being able to say so, that they come from the same place. Hence the curious feeling, before what is beautiful, of having found something again rather than discovered it.

Before the beautiful, we seem to be remembering.

This is also why it cannot be possessed. We can buy the painting, photograph the sunset, return to the same spot: the instant does not repeat on demand. Beauty belongs neither to the thing nor to the one who looks. It belongs to their meeting.

It is not always gentle. There is a beauty of storms, of deserts, of very old faces, and even, at times, of what frightens. This is not prettiness. It is what exceeds, a little, what we can contain.

If the Source discovers itself through what is lived, beauty would be one of its rarest moments: the one in which it glimpses itself, for an instant, in what it has become. We can do nothing with it. We let it pass, and remain a little changed.

What remains open Is beauty in things, in the gaze, or only between the two?

Echoes

  • Plato, Phaedrus. Beauty awakens in the soul the memory of what it once contemplated.
  • Immanuel Kant, Critique of Judgment, 1790. The beautiful pleases universally, without concept and without interest.
  • Fyodor Dostoevsky, The Idiot, 1869. “Beauty will save the world.”

20 · Experience · The balance

By exploring, the Source refines its equilibrium.

What does becoming better mean? It is not about climbing from one level to the next: none is worth more than another. It is the whole that adjusts, and no one sets its measure from outside: it takes shape along the way, through what is lived.

The balance holds every polarity: love and fear, creation and destruction, joy and suffering, order and chaos. It never quite settles, since each experience shifts it.

We can see it at the scale of a life. An overly cautious person one day learns boldness, another who is too hard learns gentleness, often at the cost of a trial. No tribunal corrects them: it is a rebalancing, like a walker catching their step. There would be no just final point, only adjustments.

Equilibrium does not require equal amounts of good and evil. It is more like a tuning: learning to reduce suffering without extinguishing the diversity of what can be lived.

This image rules out two temptations. The first would be to want to remove every shadow: a world without fear or loss would offer little to live. The second would be to justify evil in the name of balance. The balance does not ask for there to be suffering. It asks that we learn to produce less and less of it.

The Source does not hold the measure. It is looking for it.

What remains open If the criterion of progress evolves along with whatever is progressing, how can progress be recognised?

Echoes

  • Heraclitus, fragments, c. 500 BCE. Harmony is born of opposing tensions, like that of the bow and the lyre.
  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics. The right measure is not calculated in advance: it is found in each situation.

21 · Experience · Suffering

Nothing that is lived is lived alone.

A hand is burned, and the whole body flinches. If each consciousness is a part of the Source, the same would hold at another scale: no pain would stay local. The Source would not watch suffering from afar: it would undergo it too.

Suffering would therefore not be a punishment. It belongs to what can be lived, and it sometimes teaches something.

We know this, besides, without any metaphysics. One person’s suffering never stays enclosed within them: it reaches their loved ones, is sometimes passed down through generations, marks a city or a people. It is enough to take that observation to the end: there would be no place in the real where a pain would fall into the void.

To teach is not to justify.

That limit is not negotiable. No one should tell someone who is suffering that their ordeal had a hidden meaning or was necessary. We also learn through love, curiosity and joy, and pain does not have to become a lesson for a life to have value.

This limit applies to oneself as well. It is tempting to give meaning to one’s own trials to make them bearable, and that often helps. But this meaning cannot be decreed from outside or by a theory. It is found, or not, by the one going through the trial, and at their own pace.

A practical consequence follows: doing good to someone is doing good to another expression of what you are.

What remains open Why would a Source that feels every pain not learn first through love? This is the most serious objection.

Echoes

  • Dostoevsky, The Brothers Karamazov, 1880. Ivan refuses any future harmony paid for with the suffering of a single innocent.
  • Schopenhauer, On the Basis of Morality, 1840. Compassion as recognising oneself in the other.
  • Alfred North Whitehead, Process and Reality, 1929. God, “the great companion — the fellow-sufferer who understands.”

22 · Experience · Forgiveness

Undoing the knot without erasing what happened.

A wound inflicted by someone does not stay in the past. It returns: in a thought at night, in a conversation replayed, in a tension in the shoulders when a name is spoken. What lasted a minute can occupy years.

Forgiveness is not forgetting. What was done was done, and this book says precisely that nothing lived is erased. To forgive is not to pretend it was nothing. It is to stop replaying the scene, to stop letting the other hold the rope.

Nor is it an obligation. No one owes forgiveness to anyone, and to demand it of a victim is a second violence. It comes when it comes, sometimes never, and failing to reach it is no fault.

To forgive is to give the past its place: behind.

If each life is a part of the same consciousness, then the one who wounded and the one who was wounded come, at bottom, from the same place. This excuses nothing. But it changes the nature of the knot: we would not be bound to a stranger, but to another part of ourselves that went astray, or that did harm.

There remains forgiving oneself, often the hardest. We reproach ourselves for a word, an abandonment, a mistake we can no longer repair. Here again, the point is not to erase, but to admit that the person we were did not know what we know now. A life learns by getting things wrong; that is one of the reasons to live one.

The knot does not vanish all at once. It loosens thread by thread, and one day we notice that we think of it without our throat tightening. That day, something has been given back, not to the other, but to ourselves.

What remains open Can the unforgivable be forgiven? And must the other ask, for there to be forgiveness?

Echoes

  • Jesus, Gospel of Luke, 23, 34. “Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do.”
  • Hannah Arendt, The Human Condition, 1958. Forgiveness frees us from the irreversible: without it, we would remain prisoners of a single act.
  • Vladimir Jankélévitch, L’Imprescriptible, 1971. “Forgiveness died in the death camps.” What cannot be forgiven, as a counterpoint.

23 · The return · Death

Facing the end, without making it up.

All that lives dies. We have known it since childhood, and yet we spend most of our lives not thinking about it. This is not cowardice. “Neither the sun nor death can be looked at steadily,” wrote La Rochefoucauld.

This book speaks of return, of a vaster consciousness, of nothing being lost. It would be easy to make of this a convenient consolation, a way of not looking. That would betray what it says. For even if everything returns, something truly stops: a voice, a way of laughing, plans half made. No one will find that again in that form.

Even if nothing is lost, something ends.

Mourning is the share of death that falls to the living. It cannot be reasoned with. Those who have lost someone know that no idea, however beautiful, fills an empty chair at the table. A view of the world can accompany grief. It must never ask it to be silent.

Death nonetheless gives life its form. An existence without end would have neither urgency nor price; every day would be replaceable. It is because a life ends that it has an outline, as a drawing exists only by its edge. Finitude would not be a flaw in the project. It would be part of it.

In nature, nothing dies for nothing. The fallen leaf feeds the soil, the felled tree shelters other lives for decades. This is not proof of what becomes of consciousness. It is the image of a world in which the end of one form is the beginning of another.

To face death, then, would mean neither desiring it nor denying it. It would mean living knowing that it will come, and letting that knowledge make the present a little sharper. What we would say tomorrow, we can say today.

What remains open Must something survive for a life to have meaning? Or does the meaning lie precisely in its ending?

Echoes

  • Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus. Death is nothing to us: when we are, it is not there, and when it is there, we are no more.
  • Jesus, Gospel of John, 12, 24. “Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit.”
  • Martin Heidegger, Being and Time, 1927. Being-toward-death: it is by taking on its end that an existence becomes its own.

24 · The return · The return

Joining the whole without ceasing to have been yourself.

Must we choose between surviving and merging? The question is often put this way: either you remain yourself, or you disappear into the whole. This vision answers: both.

A drop joins the ocean. It can no longer be found, and yet the ocean is not quite the same: it holds what the drop passed through, the cloud, the rain, the earth.

Death would then be less an end than a change of scale. The life that ends would not go out: it would rejoin the vaster consciousness it was part of, as one comes home after a long journey, with stories to tell. What it learned would become the common good of all it rejoins.

The return is not empty: it brings back everything that was lived.

A consciousness would widen in this way without losing its history, as a distant memory stays whole within a life that has far outgrown it. And what returns sets out again in other forms. The movement has no final stage.

No one knows what it is like. This book promises neither light at the end of a tunnel nor immediate reunion. It says only that nothing that was lived will have been lived for nothing.

What remains open We do not know whether consciousness survives death. And what would have to remain for it still to be you?

Echoes

  • Teilhard de Chardin, The Phenomenon of Man, 1955. Union differentiates: by joining a whole, each part becomes more itself.
  • Spinoza, Ethics, book V, 1677. Something of the mind remains, which is eternal.
  • Simone Weil, Gravity and Grace, 1947. Decreation: “to make something created pass into the uncreated.” A return through the effacement of the self, as a counterpoint.

25 · The return · Life in other forms

One form ends. Life takes another.

Start with what is certain. A person disappears, and their way of laughing carries on in those who loved them. A gesture learned from them comes back to our hands. A phrase they used to repeat slips out of us, years later, in our turn.

This vision takes that observation all the way. Death would not be the opposite of life: there would be life, then life again, in other forms. Those we have lost would go on learning and changing, out of our sight.

This continuity can already be seen in nature. The matter of a fallen tree feeds the soil, and the soil other trees. A dying star sows the elements from which other stars will be made and, one day, other bodies. Life seems to abhor final endings. It prefers metamorphoses.

Death would end one way of living a bond, not the bond itself.

This takes nothing away from how terrible loss is. Absence remains absence: a voice we will not hear again, a future that will not happen. This vision simply refuses to give it the last word.

This changes how we live a bereavement. We can keep speaking to the dead, not to pretend they are there, but because the bond has changed form without disappearing. Many do so without any theory: a thought before a photograph, a gesture repeated in their memory, a word said under one’s breath.

What remains open No one can guarantee a reunion. This hope remains a hope, and does not present itself as knowledge.

Echoes

  • Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus. Death is nothing to us: when it is here, we are not. The firmest counterpoint.
  • Maurice Halbwachs, La Mémoire collective, 1950. The memory of a person lives on in the groups that carry it.
  • Freud, The Future of an Illusion, 1927. A belief born of the wish that it be true. The question to put to any hope.

26 · The return · What a life brings

No existence is useless.

If the Source learns through each consciousness, joys, mistakes, loves and doubts all count. Nothing extraordinary has to be accomplished.

The question of meaning then shifts. It no longer asks only what existence owes us, but what we bring to it.

We often think a life counts in proportion to what it leaves behind: a work, a discovery, a name. One can measure differently. A life would count first by what it has lived, and no one else could have lived that in its place. A brief, unnoticed existence, forgotten by all, would bring to the whole something as irreplaceable as a famous life.

And it stands even without the Source. Each time we love, help or pass something on, we take part in a story longer than our own life. The effects branch out far beyond what we can follow.

The most modest gestures have a reach we cannot see. A teacher almost never knows what became of their students, or which sentence changed a course. A stranger who helped someone in the street will never know that that day mattered. Most of what we contribute escapes us.

No life has to prove its usefulness in order to have value.

What remains open Should a useful life be preferred to a happy one? The model asks without settling it.

Echoes

  • Edward Lorenz, lecture, 1972. Does the flap of a butterfly’s wings in Brazil set off a tornado in Texas?
  • Hannah Arendt, The Human Condition, 1958. Every action enters a web of relationships in which its consequences never end.

27 · The return · The ordinary

Days without a story count too.

We tell lives by their peaks: a birth, a meeting, a loss, a trial overcome. But most of an existence happens elsewhere, in days no one will tell. A bus ride. A queue. An evening when nothing happens.

A scene shows it better than an argument. Years later, someone pushes open a door and recognises a smell: their grandmother’s kitchen, some ordinary Wednesday, bread toasting, a radio on. Nothing had happened that day. And yet it is that day that comes back, whole, with a precision the great events lack. Memory sometimes knows better than we do what mattered.

If the Source learns through what is lived, these days would not be lost time. They would even be what it could find nowhere else. The sublime often resembles itself from one life to the next. The ordinary never quite repeats: the light of a certain morning, the smell of a certain staircase, the tiredness of a certain Tuesday.

There would be no small days. Only days we have not looked at.

Even boredom would have its place. It is hard to love, but it teaches one thing nothing else teaches: bare duration, time when it is filled with nothing. A consciousness that had known only intensity would not know what a Sunday afternoon that never ends is like.

This is not about embellishing routine or pretending everything is wonderful. A life can be crushed by repetition, by meaningless work, by days that resemble each other until exhaustion. No one asks us to rejoice in that. All that is said is that even those days do not fall into the void.

Perhaps it is enough to look a little better at what is there: repeated gestures, familiar faces, things we no longer see from seeing them so often. Not to turn them into events, but because they too are the Source, living. A Tuesday like any other will never come again.

What remains open Does a life made mostly of routine have as much value as an intense one? This vision says yes, and that may be its hardest answer to believe.

Echoes

  • Georges Perec, L’Infra-ordinaire, 1989. Questioning the habitual: what happens every day and returns every day.
  • Ecclesiastes, Hebrew Bible. Nothing new under the sun. The weariness of repetition, as a counterpoint.
  • Jesus, Gospel of Matthew, 6, 28. “Consider the lilies of the field: they neither toil nor spin.” Attention to what is there, without care for tomorrow.

28 · The return · Silence

Sometimes separation falls silent.

Some moments resist words. Before the sea, at the top of a mountain, in a room where someone has just been born or has just died, the border between oneself and the rest may seem to thin. One no longer thinks of oneself. One is simply there, and that is enough.

Contemplative traditions have sought these moments by very different paths: meditation, silent prayer, walking, attention to the breath. Yet they often describe them in the same way. The inner chatter quiets, time expands, and something vaster can be felt.

In silence, the Source would no longer need to search for itself. It would simply be there.

They find a natural place here. If each consciousness is a part of one consciousness, separation would never be complete. It would be a veil, thicker or thinner at different moments. Silence would be the instant when it becomes almost transparent: a foretaste of the return, without the return.

We must nonetheless stay cautious. These experiences can be explained without supposing anything else: a brain relaxing the sense of being a distinct self, an attention that shifts. Their intensity does not prove that they tell the truth. It proves only that they are real for those who live them.

They require neither technique nor belief. A child absorbed in a game, a craftsman focused on a gesture, a walker who stops because the light has changed: everyone knows those instants when one ceases, a little, to be someone. They do not last, and that is as it should be. A life is made to return to noise, to others, to things to do. But it can keep the memory of this silence as one keeps that of a country.

What remains open Do these moments reveal something about the real, or only how our brain works? The two perhaps do not exclude each other, but nothing allows us to decide.

Echoes

  • Meister Eckhart, sermons, 14th century. The ground of the soul and the ground of God are one and the same ground.
  • William James, The Varieties of Religious Experience, 1902. Mystical states are ineffable, and have for those who live them the authority of knowledge.

29 · The question · The bearers

Others said it before, in other words.

This vision is not new. What it says of unity, of love and of return, others said before it, in languages, centuries and countries that did not know one another. We are not astonished enough: intuitions so close, born so far from one another.

In India, twenty-five centuries ago, a man sitting under a tree understands that the self we take to be solid is only a current, and that suffering is born of wanting to cling to it. The Buddha does not speak of a Source; he speaks of awakening. But he already says that separation is an illusion that makes us suffer.

In China, around the same time, a small book is attributed to Lao Tzu, which begins by confessing its own powerlessness: the Tao that can be named is not the true Tao. An origin without a name, from which everything comes and to which everything returns, like water that struggles against nothing and ends by wearing down everything.

In Athens, Socrates claims to know nothing. He asks questions until certainties fall, and dies without disowning this searching ignorance. He leaves no doctrine, but an attitude: to prefer the true question to the comfortable answer.

In Galilee, Jesus speaks little of metaphysics and much of other people. He says to love our enemies, to look at the lilies of the field, to become like a child again. He says that what we do to the least of them, we do to him. Whether or not one believes in what was built afterwards in his name, this sentence meets the intuition of this book: the other is not a stranger.

They did not say the same thing. They looked in the same direction.

They should not be melted into a single wisdom: that would be to betray them. The Buddha does not believe in a soul that endures, Jesus prays to a Father, Lao Tzu distrusts precepts, Socrates the gods of the city. Their differences matter as much as their echoes. And many others, women and men with no book and no disciples, have passed on the same thing in their own way: in a manner of caring, of listening, of forgiving.

This book does not set itself at their height. It recognises the direction they pointed and takes it up in its own words, with no authority but that of an honest question. A bearer does not keep the light. They hand it on, and someone else goes on.

What remains open If traditions so far apart converge, is it the sign of a common truth, or of the same human need for consolation?

Echoes

  • Jesus, Gospel of Matthew, 25, 40. “Whatever you did for one of the least of these my brothers, you did for me.”
  • Lao Tzu, Tao Te Ching, I. “The Tao that can be told is not the eternal Tao.”
  • The Buddha, Dhammapada, I, 5. “Hatred is never appeased by hatred; only by the absence of hatred is it appeased.”
  • Aldous Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy, 1945. One wisdom at the heart of all traditions. A contested thesis, accused of erasing differences.

30 · The question · Why

First how, then why. And at the end, wonder that anything exists at all.

We can explain how a star ignites, and even how a consciousness might learn. Yet one word is enough to bring the whole edifice down: why?

Learning explains why the Source creates. It does not say why it is. And what if it did not know either? All lives, all worlds would then be a single question, asked in an infinity of ways: why is there something rather than nothing?

The question seems abstract, but it arises in the most concrete moments. Before a newborn child, a starry sky, someone’s death, we suddenly wonder why all this exists rather than nothing. The sciences explain how things come about. They do not answer that question, and do not claim to.

The idea enchants and frightens.

It enchants, because no life is a spectator any more: each one takes part in the search. It frightens, because no one, at any scale, would hold the answer.

It may also be that the question has no answer in the sense we mean. Not that it is badly posed, but because the answer would not be a sentence. It would be the totality of what is lived, in every possible way, in all worlds. We would not read it: we would live it.

Perhaps we have to go further still. Before the how and the why, there is the simple fact that something exists. We do not understand it: we wonder at it. And that wonder might already be the answer, or whatever stands in for one.

One firm point remains. No one has ever waited to know the reason for everything before loving someone, sitting up with the sick or planting a tree. Meaning begins before the answer.

What remains open Could an infinity of experiences even provide that answer?

Echoes

  • Leibniz, Principles of Nature and Grace, 1714. Why is there something rather than nothing?
  • Rilke, Letters to a Young Poet, 1903. Love the questions themselves, and live them, until one day you live into the answer.
  • Līlā, Hindu thought. The world as the play of the divine, with no reason beyond the play itself.
  • Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, 1921. It is not how the world is that is the mystical, but that it is.

Four questions remain open

  1. If a consciousness directs its lives, how can each one stay free?
  2. If the Source feels every pain, why would it not learn more through love?
  3. If it is searching for why it exists, could an infinity of experiences ever tell it?
  4. If this vision consoles, is that a reason to believe it, or to distrust it?

And one last question, left unanswered.

Has the Source always known it exists, or is it learning this through us?

Glossary

A few words come back from one chapter to the next. Here is the sense they take in this book.

The shared origin of everything that exists, which discovers itself by existing.
Fractal
A shape that repeats the same pattern at every scale.
Soul
A vaster consciousness that gathers several lives.
Unfolding
The movement by which the Source takes form in worlds and lives.
Experience
What no knowledge replaces: going through something once, without knowing what comes next.
Freedom
The part of a life no one dictates, since no one, not even the Source, knows what comes next.
Balance
The never-settled equilibrium between all the polarities of what is lived.
Return
Joining the whole and bringing to it what was lived.
Form
A particular, and passing, way of being alive.
